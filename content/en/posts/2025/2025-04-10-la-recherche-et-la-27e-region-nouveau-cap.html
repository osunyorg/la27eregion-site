This article is part of a series aimed at describing the transformations we are striving to bring about at the 27th Region to cultivate what we believe to be our strength and our purpose: the development of transformative skills, capabilities, and resources within public organizations; the creation and long-term facilitation of coalitions bringing together local governments, private-sector actors, and researchers from different regions, to help them make progress together on a public issue; and the mobilization of multidisciplinary groups that integrate research and action to address increasingly complex challenges and generate actionable knowledge. *** Following our first article on our relationship with our members, today we’d like to discuss the role of research. Indeed, research and the humanities and social sciences have always played an essential role at the 27th Region, but that role has evolved over time. We initially viewed it as one of the key components of our activities and programs. Today, we see it as a major area of cooperation between the world of local government and the world of research—in stark contrast to the “war on science” currently unfolding across the Atlantic, which is beginning to reach our shores… Research in our early years Launched in 2008, our first “Territoires en résidences” program focused on issues related to regional policy. We conducted immersive residencies in neighborhoods, high schools, rural train stations, local government offices, and more. It is therefore no coincidence that, through these residencies, we first crossed paths with somewhat unconventional geographers, such as Martin Vanier and Luc Gwiazdzinski —who later decided to introduce his students to this type of approach. We also began welcoming young researchers into our teams of residents, such as political scientist Lucie Bargel and sociologist and anthropologist Boris Chevrot, who was a doctoral student at the time with the Clunisois Community of Municipalities. We created a research-focused monitoring position—with the hiring of Anna Lochard—but we subsequently failed to establish a CIFRE position within the 27th Region.

FIP Explo, en résidence en mairie du 19e arrondissement (novembre 2015)

Public Innovation as a Research Topic After several years of experience, our approaches have begun to attract the attention of researchers. Between 2014 and 2017, design-based public innovation methods were the focus of a fundamental research project called FIP-EXPLO , coordinated by Frédérique Pallez, bringing together Nadia Arab, Emmanuel Coblence, Olivier Hirt, Philippe Lefebvre, Apolline Le Gall, Anna Lochard, Burcu Ozdirlik, Elsa Vivant, and Jean-Marc Weller. Institutional partners included Mines ParisTech, the University of Paris Est Marne-la-Vallée, the École Nationale Supérieure de la Création Industrielle, and the 27e Région. This research aimed to better understand new forms of public innovation and to identify their benefits and limitations. Shortly thereafter, specialized chairs were established to explore these issues in greater depth: the Chair in Public Innovation in Paris and the Chair in Transformations of Public Action at Sciences Po Lyon. Ten years later, we continue to explore public innovation with academics, such as Mathias Bejean of UPEC as part of the Les Labonautes program , and Canadian researcher Lindsay Cole of the University of Vancouver through the research program “ Pushing the Boundaries of Public Innovation. ” Toward participatory forms of research Through programs like La Transfo , we’ve come to realize that our added value lies in bringing together groups of officials—and even elected officials—from different local governments around initiatives that combine the co-production of knowledge with efforts toward transformation. The model that inspires us is that of participatory action research (PAR). Researchers are now involved in nearly all of our programs, such as Rebonds (with Magali Talandier), as well as Juristes embarqués, Nouvelles Mesures (with the Chair of Ecological Accounting), and Nouveaux Accords (with consultant-researchers Manon Loisel and Nicolas Rio). We regularly contribute to the publication of research articles and scholarly journals (design sciences, the French Evaluation Society, etc.), and we aim to contribute even more in the future. Greater cooperation between research and local governments Now that we have a better grasp of the dynamics of how these projects are put together, our goal is to promote these “hybrid” forms of research—participatory action research, as well as social R&D as promoted by our friends at Ellyx—among local governments and researchers. Things are changing on the local government side: they are seeking to bring expertise back in-house amid budgetary pressures and to reduce their dependence on large consulting firms. That is why many are trying to bring in young researchers and, in the case of the largest local governments, to collaborate with universities and research laboratories on increasingly complex challenges—particularly those related to the ecological transition—as is the case with the IRIS-E program in Rennes, the Urban Environmental Workshop Zones (ZAEU) in the Eurometropolis of Strasbourg, as well as in Nantes, Grenoble, Nancy, and in dozens of university living labs, science shops, and so on. So now, how do we move forward? The will is there, but fostering genuine cooperation between research institutions and local governments is no easy task. Much remains to be done to change perceptions, make research more accessible (especially to small communities), clear up misunderstandings, and create a shared culture between the research community and public sector stakeholders. What role could we play in this regard, here at the 27th Region? We’ve begun laying the groundwork for this effort by publishing an article in the new journal *Interlignes*, as well as by collaborating with some thirty researchers and public officials to draft an initial list of recommendations for local governments —and soon, a second list aimed at researchers. To support this movement, we must strengthen our own ties to the research community in several ways. Gaining a better understanding of the research ecosystem Today, when we work with researchers, it’s most often outside the context of their laboratories. We still have a lot to learn about research institutions and how they operate, especially in the fields of participatory science and action research. We can do this by building relationships with organizations that share our approach (POPSU, GIP EPAU, etc.), or with local GIECS. In return, we can help them further integrate issues of public transformation into their research programs, as we are currently doing with the Resyst program. At the European level, we must build closer ties with the Europe and Partnerships departments of major local governments (metropolitan areas, regions) to gain better access to European programs—including Horizon Europe and its “Science and Society” component—and explore how we can participate early on in the development of projects submitted for European funding. How to better anticipate future research programs ? We are increasingly being asked to provide ad hoc input on the “public transformation” component of research programs (for example, by INRAE and its Becreative program on reducing phosphate use, or by the University of Rennes and IRIS-E on transition policies). The challenge for us is to be involved earlier in the process, when these programs are still under discussion and we can still influence the selection of objectives and the program’s implementation details. To achieve this, we need to build relationships with the right contacts within research institutes (such as INRAE for the PEPR Forest program in the case of Tronc Commun) and universities, identify research laboratories likely to lead programs that interest us, select specific research chairs, and also engage with the higher education departments of major local governments. Gaining a better access to research funding Even though the teams in charge of citizen science at the National Research Agency view us as potential partners, participatory research remains marginal and receives little support within the institution. Furthermore, since we are not classified as a research organization, we have limited direct access to research funding, unlike certain “research” calls for proposals from other government agencies (ADEME, ANCT, etc.). Nevertheless, we must strive to better understand the mechanics of all these funding mechanisms. Finally, in the much longer term, we must continue to pursue the idea of the CIR Social, championed by Ellyx, whose objective would be to fund the applied research efforts of nonprofit organizations such as the 27th Region. What do you think? We need your advice, contacts, and ideas! Public Innovation as a Research Topic

After several years of experience, our approaches have begun to attract the attention of researchers. Toward participatory forms of research

Through programs like La Transfo (external link), we’ve come to realize that our added value lies in bringing together groups of officials—and even elected officials—from different local governments around initiatives that combine the co-production of knowledge with efforts toward transformation. The model that inspires us is that of participatory action research (PAR). Researchers are now involved in nearly all of our programs, such as Rebonds (with Magali Talandier), as well as Juristes embarqués, Nouvelles Mesures (with the Chair of Ecological Accounting), and Nouveaux Accords (with consultant-researchers Manon Loisel and Nicolas Rio). We regularly contribute to the publication of research articles and scholarly journals (design sciences, the French Evaluation Society, etc.), and we aim to contribute even more in the future.


Greater cooperation between research and local governments

Now that we have a better grasp of the dynamics of how these projects are put together, our goal is to promote these “hybrid” forms of research—participatory action research, as well as social R&D as promoted by our friends at Ellyx—among local governments and researchers. Things are changing on the local government side: they are seeking to bring expertise back in-house amid budgetary pressures and to reduce their dependence on large consulting firms. That is why many are trying to bring in young researchers and, in the case of the largest local governments, to collaborate with universities and research laboratories on increasingly complex challenges—particularly those related to the ecological transition—as is the case with the IRIS-E program (external link) in Rennes, the Urban Environmental Workshop Zones (external link)(ZAEU) in the Eurometropolis of Strasbourg, as well as in Nantes, Grenoble, Nancy, and in dozens of university living labs, science shops, and so on.


So now, how do we move forward?

The will is there, but fostering genuine cooperation between research institutions and local governments is no easy task. Much remains to be done to change perceptions, make research more accessible (especially to small communities), clear up misunderstandings, and create a shared culture between the research community and public sector stakeholders. What role could we play in this regard, here at the 27th Region? We’ve begun laying the groundwork for this effort by publishing an article in the new journal *Interlignes*, (external link) as well as by collaborating with some thirty researchers and public officials to draft an initial list of recommendations for local governments (external link)—and soon, a second list aimed at researchers.

To support this movement, we must strengthen our own ties to the research community in several ways.


Gaining a better understanding of the research ecosystem

Today, when we work with researchers, it’s most often outside the context of their laboratories. We still have a lot to learn about research institutions and how they operate, especially in the fields of participatory science and action research. We can do this by building relationships with organizations that share our approach (POPSU, GIP EPAU, etc.), or with local GIECS. In return, we can help them further integrate issues of public transformation into their research programs, as we are currently doing with the Resyst program.

At the European level, we must build closer ties with the Europe and Partnerships departments of major local governments (metropolitan areas, regions) to gain better access to European programs—including Horizon Europe and its “Science and Society” component—and explore how we can participate early on in the development of projects submitted for European funding.


How to better anticipate future research programs ?

We are increasingly being asked to provide ad hoc input on the “public transformation” component of research programs (for example, by INRAE and its Becreative (external link) program on reducing phosphate use, or by the University of Rennes and IRIS-E on transition policies). The challenge for us is to be involved earlier in the process, when these programs are still under discussion and we can still influence the selection of objectives and the program’s implementation details.

To achieve this, we need to build relationships with the right contacts within research institutes (such as INRAE for the PEPR Forest program in the case of Tronc Commun) and universities, identify research laboratories likely to lead programs that interest us, select specific research chairs, and also engage with the higher education departments of major local governments.

Gaining a better access to research funding

Even though the teams in charge of citizen science at the National Research Agency view us as potential partners, participatory research remains marginal and receives little support within the institution. Furthermore, since we are not classified as a research organization, we have limited direct access to research funding, unlike certain “research” calls for proposals from other government agencies (ADEME, ANCT, etc.). Nevertheless, we must strive to better understand the mechanics of all these funding mechanisms. Finally, in the much longer term, we must continue to pursue the idea of the CIR Social, championed by Ellyx, whose objective would be to fund the applied research efforts of nonprofit organizations such as the 27th Region.


What do you think? We need your advice, contacts, and ideas!
